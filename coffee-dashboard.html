The market in one page

The permanent structure. Refresh yearly at most.

Two species, two exchanges, one spread

Arabica (NY, ~55–60% of world supply): higher quality, higher altitude, frost- and drought-sensitive. Brazil dominates, then Colombia, Ethiopia, Honduras. Robusta (London): hardier, more caffeine, instant-coffee and espresso-blend workhorse. Vietnam is the giant, Brazil's conilon second, then Indonesia and Uganda. Roasters substitute between them at the margin, so the arabica–robusta spread is a real economic dial, not just a chart — when arabica gets expensive, blends quietly get more robusta, and vice versa.

The biennial cycle — coffee's master rhythm

Arabica trees alternate on-years and off-years: a heavy crop exhausts the tree, the next year yields less. Brazil's whole national output breathes in this two-year rhythm, and every balance forecast starts from which phase Brazil is in. 2026/27 is a record on-year; 2027/28 is the biennial down-leg — which is why an El Niño arriving exactly now is taken so seriously: it would stack a weather shock on top of a biological down-year.

The weather windows

Flowering (florada), Sep–Nov: the first spring rains after Brazil's dry winter trigger the blossom; failed or staggered rains mean fewer, uneven cherries — the next crop's ceiling is set in these weeks. Frost, Jun–Aug: the classic tail event (July 2021 → the 2022 spike); one cold night can kill years of tree growth. El Niño actually reduces frost risk (warmer winters) while raising heat/drought risk — it trades one tail for another.

Free market, currency-driven

Unlike cocoa's West African boards, Brazil is a free market: farmers and co-ops are fully price-exposed, sell when they choose, and hold inventory as a trading position. Consequence one: the BRL matters enormously — producers earn dollars and pay costs in reais, so a weak real triggers origin selling and caps rallies. Consequence two: origin selling behaviour is a live sentiment indicator — Brazilian farmers hoard into strength and dump into weakness like any trader.

Demand: the steadiest in commodities

Coffee consumption grows ~1.5–2%/yr through almost anything — habit-driven, caffeine-anchored, with China and Asia compounding on top. It did not crack under record 2025 prices the way cocoa demand did; retail pass-through is slow and drinkers grumble but pour another cup. Demand is the boring, reliable line; virtually all the action in coffee balances is supply.

Structurally thin balance

World ending stocks run around 25 M bags against ~165 M of use — roughly 8 weeks of consumption, versus cocoa's ~15. Coffee lives permanently closer to the edge, which is why single weather events move it 15% in a day and why the frost/florada calendar is traded so hard.

Standing policy & structural variables

Slow variables. Refresh quarterly.

The US–Brazil tariff wedge

The 50% tariff on Brazilian coffee imports (2025, still in force) hit a flow that supplied roughly a third of US green coffee. Effects running through the system: US buyers substituting Colombia and other origins (their differentials firm), Brazilian coffee rerouting to Europe and Asia at discounts, certified stocks draining as an alternative US supply source, and US retail coffee inflation. Any negotiation headline is an instant repricing event across certs, diffs and the KC curve.

EUDR — same wall as cocoa

Coffee is in scope of the EU Deforestation Regulation taking effect for large operators at end-2026: geolocation-verified, deforestation-free sourcing into the EU. Brazil's big co-ops are relatively well prepared; the compliance pain concentrates in smallholder origins — Ethiopia, parts of Vietnam, Uganda — where a two-tier (compliant vs non-compliant) market and diverted flows are the likely outcome.

No boards, but a central bank

Brazil has no cocoa-style marketing board — the state variable is the BRL. Real weakness = origin selling pressure on rallies; real strength = farmers hold. Watching USD/BRL alongside KC is not optional; it's half the price action on quiet days.

Vietnam's quiet transformation

Record export revenues are financing replanting, irrigation and a shift of some acreage to durian and other fruit — robusta supply is reliable now but the acreage competition is a slow structural headwind worth an annual check.

Bias corrections for this market

Opposite polarity to West Africa: Brazilian producers are fully price-exposed and hold inventory, so they have every incentive to talk the crop down — treat producer pessimism like US-farmer crop talk (de-mean it, demand verifiable specifics).
